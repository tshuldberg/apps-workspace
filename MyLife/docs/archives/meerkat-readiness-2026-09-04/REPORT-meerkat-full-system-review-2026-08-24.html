The one-paragraph verdict

Meerkat is real, and it is unusually honest about itself.

The private community layer, a Discord-and-Slack-shaped product sitting on genuine end-to-end encryption that no mainstream competitor ships, is code-complete, tested to roughly 11,000 passing tests, and could launch the day you deploy a relay and clear an iOS build through TestFlight. The cryptography is coherent, well-engineered classical end-to-end encryption, materially better than almost any indie "encrypted" app. What it is not yet is the 2026 state of the art (Signal's post-quantum PQXDH, Apple's PQ3), and there is one high-severity bootstrap weakness worth fixing before launch. The remaining work is overwhelmingly operational: deploy the servers, engage a lawyer, register with NCMEC, finish the App Store build, and collect the live and two-device evidence that no test can substitute for. Two product pillars from your larger vision, the TikTok-style consumption feed and creator monetization, plus commerce and VPN, are not started in product terms, but the foundation under them is excellent.

What Meerkat actually is

Here is the machine as it exists in the repository, the foundation the full ambition builds on. Meerkat is an app that turns your phone into a small, private, encrypted server. On that device it can create content, seal it (encrypt it so only intended people can read it), sign it (prove you authored it), store it, and hand it to other people's devices over whatever connection is available. It is deliberately built as a thin, honest skin over a real cryptographic engine. The app itself contains no cryptography at all; every sensitive operation is delegated to a shared package called @mylife/sync, which is the actual substance of the project.

Three pieces make up the whole:

  • The mobile app (apps/meerkat, Expo/React Native) and its web twin (apps/meerkat-web, Next.js). These are kept in near-perfect lockstep: the pure logic lives in shared "core" files that are byte-identical across both, and a parity script fails the build if they ever drift.
  • The sync engine (packages/sync): identity, encryption, the handshake, group keys, the sealed-content store, the transport ladder, and the offline mailbox. This is ~397 TypeScript files and the heart of the system.
  • The relay platform (packages/meerkat-relay): the server side. It is really two things wearing one name, a tiny "dumb" message forwarder that sees only ciphertext, and a much larger stateful backend (community nodes, a public directory, a content archive with malware scanning, a push gateway, billing, a media seeder, and more).

The defining design decision, repeated everywhere in the code, is honesty as an architectural invariant. The app is built so that it structurally cannot lie to you about connectivity. It never shows "delivered" unless a signed receipt came back. It never shows a peer count it cannot prove. There is a file, capability-status.ts, whose entire job is to derive the app's own live/partial/pending self-assessment from real runtime flags, so the marketing surface cannot outrun the engineering. This review's audit largely agreed with that file, and in a few places found the app underclaiming features it actually has.

The genuine advantages

These are the things Meerkat does that are real, verified in code, and that most of its would-be competitors do not do at all.

1. End-to-end encryption that most social apps simply do not have

TikTok, Reddit, X, Discord, YouTube, Patreon: none of these encrypt your content end-to-end. They cannot; their business is reading it. Meerkat's content is sealed on your device before it ever moves, and the servers that carry it hold only ciphertext. That is a category difference, not a feature difference.

2. A relay that is genuinely blind, and provably so

The core relay is a "dumb pipe". It forwards sealed frames verbatim without ever decoding them. It never learns who you are, who you are talking to, or what you said. It sees only opaque tokens that rotate daily, message sizes, and timing. This is enforced by construction (the relay has no code path to decrypt) and locked by automated privacy tests. On the identity axis this is actually stronger than Signal's Sealed Sender, which still knows the recipient account.

3. A two-identity wall that a court order cannot easily collapse

To fight spam and verify humanness without deanonymizing you, Meerkat splits identity into a "verification account" (Sign in with Apple or Google, which knows only that you are a real, of-age, paying human) and your actual mesh identity (a device key the account service never sees). Between them sits an anonymous blind-signed credential: the server signs a token it cannot read, so it can vouch that you are verified without knowing which activity is yours. A CI test statically inspects every database table and fails the build if any table ever puts an account ID next to a device or persona ID.

4. Content trust that does not depend on trusting the host

Every piece of content is addressed by a cryptographic hash of its own contents and signed by its author. When your device fetches something from a stranger's server, it verifies the signature and the hash before storing it. A malicious host can waste your bandwidth but cannot feed you forged or tampered content. This is the property that makes a future community-hosted, torrent-style network safe by default.

5. MySpace-grade customization, already built

The theme system is a real, shippable feature: a visual theme editor, shareable theme "blobs" over deep links and QR codes, per-community reskinning that a community owner controls, automatic contrast enforcement for accessibility, and light/dark following. This is the closest pillar of your vision to done.

6. Honest offline delivery and encrypted-at-rest media libraries

The "mailbox" lets a peer park an encrypted message for you while you are offline, and your device only opens envelopes that verify against your key (anything forged is silently dropped and counted). The library ("Plex-style") stores media encrypted at rest, streams it through an on-device decrypting server so plaintext never lands on disk, and structurally prevents a community from learning what you watched.

The through-line: Meerkat's advantage is not any single feature. It is that the entire stack is built on the assumption that no server, and no other user, should be trusted, and then it makes the honest-by-construction UI a selling point rather than a constraint.

How the cryptography works

You asked for the real thing, in plain language first and then with the technical names where they carry meaning. Here is what actually happens when two Meerkat devices talk.

Establishing identity

Every device generates two key pairs the first time it runs: an Ed25519 signing key (its permanent identity; the public half is the device's ID) and an X25519 key for key agreement. These are stored in the operating system keychain (iOS Keychain, Android Keystore) as encrypted bytes. When you add a friend, their signed "identity bundle" is pinned on first contact, and any later change to their key raises a warning instead of silently trusting the new one. This is trust-on-first-use (TOFU), the same model SSH uses. For higher assurance you can compare a five-emoji short authentication string (SAS) out loud, which is Meerkat's equivalent of Signal's safety numbers.

Setting up a session

When a real sync session opens, several layers stack up. First, every frame on the wire is wrapped in a pairwise frame envelope: a fresh random nonce plus an authenticated encryption of the whole frame, keyed from the shared secret the two devices established when they paired. This is the layer that blinds the relay. Inside that, the two devices run a signed challenge-response handshake to prove they hold their identity keys. Then they run a real Noise NK handshake, which mixes fresh ephemeral keys to produce a session key that only exists for this one session. This is what gives forward secrecy: even if someone later steals your device keys, they cannot decrypt sessions you already finished, because the ephemeral keys were thrown away.

A notable strength: forward secrecy here is mandatory and fails closed. If the ephemeral leg does not complete, the session dies rather than quietly falling back to a weaker mode. Encryption is required by default; plaintext is only possible if both sides explicitly and mutually opt out on the wire. Every downgrade path in the code fails the session and records an honest failed row. This is the correct posture and it is rare to see it done this carefully.

Encrypting each message

Content is encrypted with XSalsa20-Poly1305 (the nacl.secretbox construction), an authenticated cipher with a 24-byte random nonce per message, so nonce reuse is not a practical concern. Keys are derived with a real HKDF over HMAC-SHA-512. Within a session there is a per-message key chain (a "ratchet") so each message gets its own key. Signatures on data batches are Ed25519 and mandatory: a missing signature is rejected exactly like a forged one.

Groups, sealing, and anonymity

Communities use epoch keys: the group shares a symmetric key for the current "epoch", and when someone is added or removed the key rotates so a removed member cannot read anything new. Membership is the read capability. Stored library media uses a per-object key wrapped under that epoch key. And the anonymous credential is a correctly-implemented RSABSSA blind signature (RFC 9474, RSA-2048, PSS with SHA-512), the piece that lets the server vouch for you without identifying you.

The cryptography gaps, ranked

These are the specific distances between "solid classical E2EE" and the "advanced cryptography at launch" bar you want to be able to claim under expert scrutiny.

HIGH · fix before launch  The QR pairing handshake is unauthenticated.

When two devices pair via QR or code, the payload that carries the key material is not signed, and the six-digit code shown is decorative (never cryptographically verified; the validator is just a format check). Whoever controls the pairing channel at that moment can substitute their own key under your friend's ID, and every later defense keys off the attacker's secret. The workspace-pairing variant is worse: it ships literal placeholder strings where signatures should be. This is the trust root of everything else, and it is also the most fixable item. The signed-bundle machinery already exists elsewhere in the code; the fix is to route pairing through it and make the SAS comparison mandatory. identity/pairing.ts

STRATEGIC  No post-quantum component anywhere.

Every key agreement is X25519, every signature Ed25519, the credential is RSA-2048. Signal and iMessage both ship hybrid post-quantum. Every session and every stored sealed share is exposed to "harvest now, decrypt later". The good news: the handshake is a real Noise SymmetricState, so adding an ML-KEM-768 hybrid leg is a local change at one insertion point, and the protocol already carries version negotiation to roll it out. Recommended library: @noble/post-quantum (runs on Hermes).

MEDIUM  No DH ratchet, and within-session keys are never deleted.

Forward secrecy exists at session granularity, but inside a session the "ratchet" retains every chain key and message key (kept to tolerate out-of-order delivery). Capture the live session state and you decrypt the whole session. There is no post-compromise recovery: a stolen pair key never self-heals. Short-term fix is to delete message keys after use; the real fix is a Double Ratchet layered over the session key, for which all the primitives are already present.

MEDIUM  No hardware-backed keys.

Private keys are generated in JavaScript and stored as hex strings in the keychain, then loaded into app memory on every use. On iOS the bytes are protected by the Keychain and are non-migratable, which is reasonable for a v1, but nothing lives in the Secure Enclave, there is no biometric gate on key use, and no key attestation. iMessage keeps its keys in the Secure Enclave. Describe this as "keys in the OS keychain", never as "hardware-backed".

LOWER  Also worth scheduling: a legacy non-standard key-derivation mode still lingers as a fallback on two channels; the whole crypto foundation is hand-rolled pure-JavaScript on top of the now-unmaintained tweetnacl (correct on read-through, but a large self-audited surface; migrating to the maintained, audited @noble/* family is advisable); the pairwise root secret is stored raw and never rotated; and the verification numbers (5-emoji SAS, 8-hex fingerprint) are weaker than Signal's for offline comparison.

Security findings across the whole system, ranked

Beyond the cryptography itself, the transport, relay, and account layers surfaced these. None are remote "read everyone's plaintext" breaks; the confidentiality and integrity story holds. But several should be closed before a public deployment.

HIGH Relay trusts x-forwarded-for unconditionally A directly-connected attacker can set an arbitrary IP header per socket and bypass every per-client rate and connection cap, reopening flood vectors the design otherwise closes. LOW Dead scaffolding in the sync package An older "ambient" transport ladder has a LAN rung whose send() discards bytes, and the BitTorrent-style swarm classes are pure bookkeeping with no peer wire protocol. No security impact, but drift risk and misleading if presented as complete. Wire the real LAN adapter in or delete the mock; decide whether the torrent scaffolding becomes real swarming or gets retired.

What the audit did not find is as important as what it did. No plaintext leaks to the relay. No silent encryption downgrade. No forged-data acceptance (inbound policy is a pure, exhaustively-tested, fail-closed decision function applied inside a transaction with a full audit trail). No SQL injection (strict identifier whitelisting). No fabricated UI states. The hard security invariants hold.

The relay is well-hardened as a public endpoint: hello-first enforcement, strict schema validation, size caps, per-IP connection and rate caps that survive reconnects, and global caps on every resource. Identity and the privacy wall

Meerkat actually runs three identities, layered so that the outer one can vouch for the inner ones without linking them:

  1. Device identity (the inner, real you): the Ed25519/X25519 keypair used for pairing, DMs, communities, and sync.
  2. Public persona (the middle): a separate key for the public tier, in its own secret-store namespace and its own signature domain, so a persona signature can never be mistaken for a device signature.
  3. Verification account (the outer): the Apple/Google sign-in that holds only "is a real, of-age, entitled human", and issues the anonymous blind credential.

The audit confirmed the wall holds in code for the normal path: an automated test parses every database migration and fails if any table joins an account ID to a persona or device ID; account secrets live in a separate keychain service; and no account identifier appears anywhere in mesh storage. The private mesh (DMs, communities, sync) never touches the account layer at all.

The one honest asterisk: the credential is unconditionally unlinkable only if you mint it and let it expire. If you renew (or delete-and-burn), you present the expiring credential to the account service under an authenticated session, and it can compute that credential's serial. So the anonymity is "one epoch forward unlinkable", not "unconditionally unlinkable", for anyone who renews. This is also the exact mechanism that makes banning an abusive anonymous user possible at all. It is a reasonable trade, but a code comment currently overstates it, and that should be corrected.

The censorship-resistance vs safety tension, honestly

This is the hardest strategic question in the whole product, and the code has already made a defensible choice. The private tier is provably unscanned (the content-scanning function literally never executes for private scopes, proven by a counting test), which is structurally identical to Signal or WhatsApp, and defensible on the same grounds: user reporting plus metadata-level enforcement. The public tier, where you host media for strangers, is where legal exposure concentrates, and every serious control point (malware scanning, a moderation queue, an NCMEC reporting queue, DMCA intake, takedown propagation, a community kill switch) already exists as code on operated infrastructure.

Which means the honest truth is: the network is not "uncontrolled" at the public layer; it is controlled by whoever runs those nodes, which is you. The vision of a fully uncontrolled host and the reality of operating the choke points cannot both be true, and the code has correctly chosen to be operable. As a US-operated public archive, you inherit mandatory CSAM reporting duties the moment you know of such content, plus EU/UK obligations for public surfaces. The safety machinery is built, but it is seams awaiting activation: the NCMEC filing is currently a manual export, the abuse hash database is empty by default, no DMCA agent is registered, and no trust-and-safety authority key is provisioned. Launching public media hosting before those are real, not just coded, is the single largest legal risk in the product.

The path to production, and where our next conversation goes

You said the goal after this review is to refine every aspect of Meerkat toward launch. Based on everything above, here is how I would sequence the refinement, and these are the threads we should pull on together next.

Before launch (blocking)

  • Fix the pairing authentication (HIGH crypto finding). Small, local, and it is the trust root. This is the one cryptographic change I would insist on shipping first.
  • Fix the relay x-forwarded-for trust and the account-deletion tombstone before any public relay or billing is live.
  • Deploy the relay fleet + Postgres data plane and flip the default relay URL. Nothing about the app is real to a user until this exists. This is the highest-leverage single action.
  • Finish the EAS/TestFlight build (bake the RC key and relay URL, submit) and run the deferred two-device QA on calls, rooms, background sync, and in-person pairing.
  • Activate the safety machinery for real if the public tier launches: NCMEC registration and filing, DMCA agent, a populated abuse-hash set, and a staffed trust-and-safety inbox. Or launch the private community layer first and hold the public archive until this is genuinely operable.
