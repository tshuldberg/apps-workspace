# U.S. Fiscal Comparison: Immigration, Health Care, Bailouts, and High-Income Tax Preferences

**Evidence cutoff:** 2026-08-30

**Geography:** United States, with comparable international evidence where available

**Dollar convention:** Current U.S. dollars unless labeled otherwise
**Period convention:** The requested windows overlap in 2020. This report preserves that overlap. "All time" means the full source series or the lifetime of a named program, not the history of the United States.

## Executive finding

There is no defensible single total that can be split into "legal immigration cost," "illegal immigration cost," medical spending, corporate bailouts, bank bailouts, and tax breaks for people making more than $5 million. The underlying ledgers measure different things. Some are purchases, some are transfers, some are loans that were repaid, some are contingent guarantees, some are revenue estimates against a hypothetical tax baseline, and some are net fiscal effects after taxes.

The evidence supports a comparison only after preserving those distinctions:

| Domain | Best-supported result | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Recent U.S. immigration surge, federal, 2024-2034 | **$1.2T revenue, $0.3T mandatory spending plus interest, $0.9T deficit reduction** | A projection for the 2021-2026 surge population, not all immigrants and not an unauthorized-only estimate. |
| Recent U.S. immigration surge, state and local, 2023 | **$19.3B direct spending, $10.1B taxes, $9.2B net cost** | An incremental estimate for the surge population. CBO's broader potential estimate is $28.6B spending, $18.8B revenue, and $9.8B net cost. |
| U.S. national health expenditure, 2024 | **$5.279T, $15,474 per person, 18.0% of GDP** | Spending by all payers, not federal spending alone. |
| U.S. health expenditure, 2012-2020 | **$30.266T nominal; $35.641T in 2024 health-price dollars** | Nine calendar years, including 2020. |
| U.S. health expenditure, 2020-2024 | **$23.372T nominal; $24.569T in 2024 health-price dollars** | Five calendar years, including 2020. |
| TARP, program lifetime | **$443.5B disbursed; $31.1B net fiscal cost** | Gross financing was about 14 times the final net cost. Bank capital purchases produced a $16.3B net gain. |
| Paycheck Protection Program, 2020-2021 | **More than $799B in loans; about $760B forgiven by July 2024** | Mostly a grant-like business and payroll support program, not a bank equity rescue. |
| Returns with adjusted gross income above $5M, 2023 | **79,644 returns; $1.244T AGI; $326.3B income tax before credits** | IRS return counts are not unique people and do not include unrealized gains. |
| Illustrative realized capital-income rate preference for returns above $5M, 2023 | **$87.1B** | Author calculation, not an official tax-expenditure estimate. It applies the top ordinary-versus-preferential rate gap to qualified dividends plus net long-term capital gains. |

The central substantive result is that health care operates on a much larger annual scale than the other categories. Immigration is the category for which a status-separated national total is least supportable. Bailout headlines are most distorted when peak credit exposure is reported as taxpayer cost. High-income tax preferences can be estimated, but only against an explicit counterfactual and without pretending that unrealized wealth is annual income.

## 1. Measurement rules

### 1.1 Quantities that must remain separate

| Measure | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Gross expenditure or transfer | Cash paid without subtracting receipts | National health expenditure; forgiven PPP principal |
| Credit exposure | Maximum or committed loans, guarantees, or asset purchases | Federal Reserve emergency lending |
| Net fiscal cost | Outlays and subsidy costs less repayments, fees, dividends, and recoveries | Final TARP cost |
| Tax expenditure | Estimated revenue loss relative to a specified tax baseline | Exclusion of employer health premiums |
| Net fiscal effect | Taxes and contributions less benefits and public services | OECD immigrant fiscal-impact estimates |

Adding across these rows would double count and create a number with no coherent economic meaning. For example, employer health premiums are already part of national health expenditure. The tax exclusion subsidizes the financing of those premiums, so adding the tax expenditure to health spending would count the same health coverage twice.

### 1.2 Evidence hierarchy

This review prioritizes administrative data and estimates from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), Internal Revenue Service (IRS), U.S. Treasury, Federal Reserve, Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), Government Accountability Office (GAO), Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT), Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), World Health Organization (WHO), International Monetary Fund (IMF), World Bank, and Eurostat. Advocacy estimates are excluded from the quantitative core because their immigration-status classifications, counterfactuals, and household-incidence rules are not consistently comparable.

## 2. Immigration

### 2.1 Why a legal-versus-unauthorized national total does not exist

No federal budget ledger classifies every tax receipt, benefit, school cost, hospital payment, public good, or U.S.-born child's expenditure by the immigration status of an adult household member. The categories also do not map cleanly onto a binary:

- Naturalized citizens are legal immigrants but are citizens in most administrative data.
- Lawful permanent residents, refugees, people granted asylum, temporary workers, students, parolees, and applicants with pending cases have different eligibility rules.
- CBO's recent category of "other foreign nationals" includes people with and without lawful status. It cannot be renamed "illegal immigrants."
- U.S.-born children are citizens even when one or both parents lack lawful status.
- Unauthorized residents generally cannot receive most federal benefits, but emergency Medicaid and some state-funded services remain available, while eligible citizen household members may receive benefits.
- Many unauthorized workers pay sales, property, payroll, and income taxes. Some payroll taxes are credited to records that may never produce benefits.

CBO warned in 2007 that state and local studies commonly found costs above tax receipts for unauthorized immigrants, but also found that methods were too inconsistent to produce a national aggregate. That limitation remains material.

### 2.2 Best official U.S. estimates

| Population and period | Spending or outlay | Revenue | Net | What the estimate answers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Immigration surge, federal, 2024-2034 | $0.3T mandatory outlays plus interest | $1.2T | **-$0.9T deficit** | CBO counterfactual for the above-baseline 2021-2026 surge. Negative means deficit reduction. |
| Surge population, federal direct taxes and mandatory benefits, 2024-2034 | $177B mandatory benefits | $788B income and payroll tax | **+$611B direct balance** | A narrower direct subtotal. It excludes broader economic feedback and discretionary spending. |
| Surge population, state and local, 2023 direct | $19.3B | $10.1B | **$9.2B cost** | Direct spending and taxes attributable to the surge. |
| Surge population, state and local, 2023 broader potential | $28.6B | $18.8B | **$9.8B cost** | Adds possible secondary spending and revenues. |
| Immigration-specific federal discretionary funding, 2024 | $37B | Not paired | Not a net measure | Border, immigration processing, and related agency funding identified by CBO. |
| Unauthorized work and Social Security, 2010 | $1B benefits | $13B payroll taxes | **$12B cash-flow gain** | Older Social Security-specific estimate, not the total fiscal effect of unauthorized immigration. |

The federal and state-local results are not contradictory. Federal finances collect income and payroll taxes and bear relatively limited near-term benefit costs for many recent arrivals. State and local governments bear more immediate school, shelter, and emergency-service costs. The same migration can therefore improve the federal balance while increasing local costs.

CBO also estimated that, within the federal 2024-2034 surge projection, premium tax credits add $59B, other refundable tax credits add $43B, and Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program add $40B. These are included in CBO's mandatory-spending accounting where the relevant eligibility rules apply. They are not an unauthorized-only "tax break" total.

### 2.3 Requested period summary

| Window | Legal immigration total | Unauthorized immigration total | Defensible conclusion |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Annual | Not available | Not available | Use the 2023 surge estimate and the 2024 immigration-specific appropriation as bounded annual anchors, not national status totals. |
| 2012-2020 | Not available | Not available | No official comprehensive, non-overlapping national status ledger exists. |
| 2020-present | Not available | Not available | CBO's 2021-2026 surge work is the strongest official evidence, but its population is not equivalent to unauthorized residents. |
| All time | Not estimable | Not estimable | Any precise total would be model-dependent and would mix descendants, public goods, taxes, and changing legal categories. |

### 2.4 International comparison

The OECD compared the net fiscal contribution of the foreign-born population in 25 countries using a common 2006-2018 framework. It did not separate legal and unauthorized immigrants. Two specifications show how strongly the result depends on the treatment of public goods:

- **Specification A:** taxes and social contributions less individualized health, education, and social-protection spending.
- **Specification C2:** the same calculation after allocating all public goods to people on a per-capita basis.

| Country | A: individualized net, % GDP | C2: all public goods, % GDP |
|---|---:|---:|
| Luxembourg | 7.64 | 2.89 |
| Norway | 1.34 | 1.22 |
| Portugal | 1.79 | 0.89 |
| Switzerland | 3.18 | 0.84 |
| Italy | 1.87 | 0.57 |
| United Kingdom | 2.02 | 0.23 |
| Greece | 1.24 | 0.04 |
| United States | 1.00 | -0.68 |
| France | 1.02 | -0.85 |
| Belgium | 1.38 | -1.28 |
| Latvia | 0.28 | -1.58 |
| 25-country average | 1.56 | -0.16 |

Under the individualized specification, foreign-born residents made a positive net contribution in every country. After allocating national defense, debt service, and other public goods per capita, many estimates became slightly negative. Most total effects remained within roughly -1% to +1% of GDP. This is the most important international result: methodology changes the sign more often than country differences do.

### 3.4 Health tax preferences

The largest health tax preference is the exclusion of employer-paid medical insurance and care from taxable employee income.

| Measure | Amount | Treatment in this report |
|---|---:|---|
| Treasury income-tax expenditure, FY2026 | $295.66B | Report separately from health spending. |
| Treasury payroll-tax effect, FY2026 | $182.58B | May be combined with the same exclusion's income-tax effect. |
| Combined FY2026 effect | **$478.24B** | A revenue-loss estimate, not additional medical care. |
| Combined projected effect, FY2026-2035 | **$6.095T** | $3.813T income-tax plus $2.282T payroll-tax effect. Projection, not observed spending. |

Treasury cautions that tax expenditures generally cannot be summed across provisions because provisions interact, taxpayers change behavior, and the estimates use a specified baseline. The combined employer-coverage figure is narrower: it combines two tax bases for one exclusion, consistent with CBO's approach. It still must not be added to national health expenditure.

## 4. Corporate and bank rescues

### 4.1 U.S. program accounting

| Program | Gross support or exposure | Recoveries or final treatment | Net fiscal result | Classification |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Federal Reserve 2008 crisis facilities | About $1.5T peak credit outstanding | Loans repaid; facilities earned fees and interest | No comparable taxpayer loss reported for the lending portfolio | Peak credit exposure, not cumulative cost |
| Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), lifetime | $443.5B disbursed | $425.5B collected, or $443.1B including additional AIG proceeds; $13.1B interest expense | **$31.1B cost** | Treasury investment and subsidy program |
| TARP bank Capital Purchase Program | $204.9B disbursed | Repayments, dividends, and warrants | **$16.3B gain** | Bank equity support |
| Paycheck Protection Program | More than $799B in guaranteed loans | About $760B forgiven by July 2024 | Forgiveness is the dominant transfer; exact lifetime net cost differs from principal forgiven | Business and payroll grant-like support |
| Airline Payroll Support Program | $59B distributed | Treasury received $15B in notes plus warrants; more than half of notes remained outstanding in April 2026 | About **$52B booked subsidy/cash cost** across rounds, on different accounting bases | Industry payroll support |
| 2020 Federal Reserve emergency facilities | $114B Treasury invested; $215B pledged | Facilities wound down and proceeds returned | **$0.749B net gain** to Treasury and the Fed through September 2025 | Credit backstop |
| Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank systemic-risk actions | $16.7B estimated Deposit Insurance Fund cost at September 2025 | Bank-industry special assessment | **$0 general-fund taxpayer cost by design** | Deposit insurance resolution |
| First Republic resolution | $16.7B estimated Deposit Insurance Fund loss in 2023 | Funded by risk-based bank premiums | Not a general-fund bailout | Bank resolution |

The popular $7.7T, $16T, or $24T descriptions of the 2008 Federal Reserve response generally add repeated short-term loans every time they rolled over. The Federal Reserve's approximately $1.5T peak outstanding balance is the defensible exposure measure. It is still not a taxpayer-cost measure.

### 4.3 International comparison

Eurostat's April 2026 accounting covers direct support to distressed financial institutions from 2007-2025. It reports:

- Cumulative net deficit effects above 10% of 2025 GDP in Cyprus and Greece.
- Effects between 5% and 10% in Ireland, Portugal, and Slovenia.
- Effects between 1% and 5% in Spain, Latvia, Austria, and Germany.
- Net cumulative gains in Luxembourg, Denmark, Belgium, Sweden, and France after fees, interest, dividends, and asset sales.
- Remaining bank-rescue-related government debt of EUR383.4B, or 2.0% of EU GDP, at the end of 2025.
- Remaining contingent liabilities of EUR54.4B, or 0.3% of EU GDP, at the end of 2025.

For pandemic response, the IMF's September 2021 snapshot separates above-the-line spending and forgone revenue from liquidity measures such as equity, loans, and guarantees. This is broader than corporate bailouts because it includes people, health, and firms.

|---|---:|---:|
| United States | 25.50 | 2.44 |
| United Kingdom | 19.27 | 16.72 |
| Australia | 18.37 | 1.78 |
| Japan | 16.73 | 28.33 |
| Canada | 15.92 | 3.95 |
| Germany | 15.32 | 27.79 |
| Italy | 10.90 | 35.25 |

The comparison shows why cash and credit must be separated. Italy and Japan announced large liquidity envelopes relative to GDP, while the United States relied much more heavily on above-the-line fiscal support. Announced capacity is not the same as utilization or final cost.

| Policy | Estimate | Scope |

The 2017 corporate rate cut is not normally listed as a tax expenditure because it changed the statutory baseline itself. It is still a tax cut, and JCT's legislation score is the appropriate measure. That score cannot be added to later annual tax-expenditure tables without overlap.

The IRS Statistics of Income tables report tax returns by adjusted gross income (AGI). This review combines the $5M-under-$10M and $10M-or-more bins. ### 6.4 What the illustrative preference does and does not measure

The author estimate applies this formula to the two IRS income lines:

`(top ordinary income-tax rate - top long-term capital-gains rate) x (qualified dividends + net long-term capital gains)`

The rate gap is 20 percentage points in 2012, 19.6 points in 2013-2017, and 17 points in 2018-2023. The estimate is transparent and reproducible, but it is not an IRS, Treasury, or JCT tax-expenditure number. It assumes the reported income would otherwise face the top ordinary rate and ignores taxpayer behavior, timing, loss netting, special 28% gain categories, deferral, unrealized gains, step-up in basis at death, charitable contributions, and interactions with other provisions. The 3.8% net investment income tax applies to both sides of this narrow comparison and therefore does not change the rate gap.

Raw itemized deductions are shown as exposure, not as tax savings. Multiplying or adding them to the rate-preference estimate would overstate the result because deductions have different marginal values and interact with limits and other rules.

Treasury's economy-wide FY2026 estimate for the capital-gains preference is $135.34B. It covers all taxpayers and uses Treasury's tax-expenditure baseline. The $87.1B figure above covers only realized qualified dividends and net long-term gains reported on returns above $5M in 2023 and uses a simpler rate-difference counterfactual.

## 7. Cross-category comparison

The most informative comparison uses current annual flows and clearly labeled one-time programs:

| Item | Amount | Year or horizon | Comparable as annual spending? |
|---|---:|---|---|
| U.S. national health expenditure | $5.279T | 2024 | Yes |
| Employer health-insurance tax exclusion, income plus payroll | $478.2B | FY2026 estimate | No, it subsidizes part of the $5.279T-type health flow and should not be added to it |
| Immigration-specific federal discretionary funding | $37B | 2024 | Yes, but it is not the total fiscal effect of immigration |
| State-local net cost of recent immigration surge | $9.2B-$9.8B | 2023 | Yes, for the surge estimate only |
| Illustrative realized capital-income rate preference, AGI above $5M | $87.1B | 2023 | Yes as an author counterfactual, not an official expenditure |
| TARP net cost | $31.1B | 2008-2023 program lifetime | No, lifetime one-time program result |
| PPP principal forgiven | About $760B | 2020-2024 cumulative | No, multi-year emergency transfer |
| 2020 Federal Reserve facilities net result | $0.749B gain | Through September 2025 | No, lifetime credit-facility result |

The annual U.S. health-care system is roughly two orders of magnitude larger than the strongest annual state-local immigration net-cost estimate and far larger than the final lifetime cost of TARP. This does not make the smaller categories unimportant. It shows that comparisons must use the same unit and time basis.

## 8. Conclusions

1. **Immigration:** A legal-versus-unauthorized national spending split is not available from official accounts. The best recent causal evidence finds a large federal fiscal gain from the immigration surge over 2024-2034 and a much smaller state-local cost in 2023. International estimates for the foreign-born population cluster near zero after all taxes and services are allocated, but the result changes with public-good accounting.
2. **Medical expenditure:** U.S. health spending is the dominant recurring flow in this comparison. It reached $5.279T in 2024 and materially exceeds peer-country spending as a share of GDP and per person.
3. **Bailouts:** Gross authorization and peak credit exposure are not taxpayer cost. TARP's $443.5B gross disbursement became a $31.1B net lifetime cost, while its bank-capital component earned money. PPP was different: forgiveness made it primarily a transfer.
4. **Corporate tax preferences:** Legislative scores and tax-expenditure estimates answer different questions. The 2017 corporate-rate provision was estimated to reduce revenue by $1.349T over 2018-2027, while the 2025 law's business-tax changes add nearly $1T to primary deficits over 2025-2034.
5. **Income above $5M:** IRS data permit an exact annual description of reported income and taxes, but not a complete measure of tax advantages. A transparent realized-rate estimate is about $1.019T over 2012-2023, with $87.1B in 2023. It excludes unrealized gains and should be read as a bounded counterfactual, not a bill sent to taxpayers.

### Calculation notes

- U.S. health period totals sum CMS annual national health expenditures. Real totals use CMS Table 23's real expenditure series rescaled from 2017 dollars to 2024 health-price dollars with the CMS health price index.
- World health totals multiply World Bank indicator `SH.XPD.CHEX.GD.ZS` by World Bank world GDP `NY.GDP.MKTP.CD` for each year, then sum the annual current-dollar results.
- IRS values combine the $5M-under-$10M and $10M-or-more AGI bins. Monetary amounts were converted from thousands to billions.
- The high-income rate-preference formula and rates are stated in Section 6.4. The estimate is not official and is deliberately not added to raw deductions or other tax-expenditure lines.
- Negative net cost means a fiscal gain or deficit reduction. Positive cost means spending exceeds identified revenue or recoveries.
