The Big Four-Day Build: A Conversation

Everything we built from July 2 to July 5, 2026, and why. Explained so a 10-year-old can follow along. One kid asks, one builder answers.

Part 1: The big picture

What even is this whole project?

It is called MyLife. Think of it as one giant toolbox of apps that one person builds. Inside there are little apps for tracking books, moods, plants, and workouts, and bigger apps too: a private chat network called Meerkat, a cooking contest app called BestChef, a gym coach app called DoWork, and a newspaper app called MyNews. The big rule for all of them: they never lie to you. If something did not really send, the app must not say "Sent."

What happened in these four days?

We wrote 234 batches of changes (each batch is called a "commit," like saving your game with a note about what you did). That added about 188,000 new lines of instructions for the computer. We also wrote 240 new files of tests. Tests are little robot checkers that try the app automatically and yell if something breaks.

Why so fast?

Because several builder teams worked at the same time, each on a different app, like four groups building different LEGO sets at different tables. At the end we carried all the sets to one big table (that is called "merging") and checked that everything still fit together.

Part 2: Meerkat, the private clubhouse network

What is Meerkat?

Imagine a clubhouse app like Discord, but private. Nobody's messages sit on a big company's computer where the company can read them. Instead, your phone and your friends' phones pass sealed envelopes to each other. Even the "post office" computer that helps carry envelopes (we call it a relay) cannot open them. It just sees locked boxes.

What did you build for it this week?

1. Private messages, finally. Meerkat could do group chats in a clubhouse, but not one-on-one private notes. That was like a school where you can talk in class but never pass a note to one friend. We built real private messages: one-on-one and small private groups. Every note is sealed so only the right person can open it.

2. Honest checkmarks. In many apps the checkmark says "delivered" even when the app is just guessing. Ours only turns on when the other phone sends back a signed proof, like a receipt with a signature that cannot be faked.

3. Changing the locks when someone leaves a group. If you kick someone out of a group note circle, they should not be able to read the NEW notes. So when someone leaves, everyone still in the group gets a brand-new secret code. We wrote a test with three pretend phones that proves the removed phone cannot read anything new.

4. Delete-for-everyone, safely. You can erase your own message from everyone's phone. But ONLY your own. Two separate guards both check "is the deleter the writer?" so nobody can erase someone else's words.

5. Really kicking someone out of a clubhouse. Before this week, the remove-member button did not truly work all the way down, which breaks our honesty rule. Now the owner signs a removal order (a signature made of math that nobody can forge), everyone's phones see it, and the locks change.

6. Making the chat feel nice. The chat worked but looked like a science experiment. We rebuilt it to feel like the chat apps you know: bubbles, emoji reactions, replies, @mentions, and new messages appearing on their own instead of you pressing Refresh like it is 2009.

7. Making it easy to join. Joining used to mean pasting a long weird code. Now a friend sends a link or shows a QR code, you scan it, see a preview card, and tap Join.

8. A feed about the actual posts. The home feed used to spend most of its space explaining HOW it works, like a TV that mostly shows its own wiring diagram. Now it shows posts, pictures, reply counts, and real profile photos. Explanations moved behind an info button.

9. Link previews that protect you. When someone shares a link, the SENDER's phone fetches the preview and sends it along. Your phone never visits a website just because someone sent it to you, so nobody can trick your phone into knocking on a stranger's door.

10. Armor for helper computers. The volunteer computers that help store clubhouse stuff got limits on how big requests can be and how much junk strangers can stuff in, so a prankster cannot fill them up like cramming a mailbox with pizza flyers.

11. A "prove you are a person" gate. For public spaces we added a humanity check, and it fails closed: if the check cannot run, the door stays shut. A broken lock should be a locked door, not an open one.

12. Clubhouses choose how their data travels. A clubhouse can say "our stuff only travels over local Wi-Fi, never the internet," and the app obeys everywhere, even refusing updates that arrive the wrong way.

13. Auto-connect. Phones now find each other and sync on their own with polite retry timers, instead of waiting for a button press.

14. Turning a clubhouse into a private Netflix. Every clubhouse is already a locked storage box, so we built shelves for it: family videos, music, and photos that members can browse and play, like your own private Netflix plus Spotify plus photo album. Every file gets its own tiny lock, and that tiny lock is itself locked with the clubhouse key. Playback runs through a tiny pretend web server INSIDE the app, so the video never leaves the sealed world. Plus photo timelines, offline maps checked piece by piece, and themes so every clubhouse looks different.

Did the inspectors find anything wrong with all that?

Yes, a few things, and that is exactly why we inspect. The biggest one: if the "you are removed" letter reaches a phone through the backup gossip path instead of the normal mailbox, that phone updates the clubhouse card but forgets to cross the removed person off its own member list. The removed person still cannot read new secrets (the locks changed), but the list is wrong, and wrong lists cause trouble later. Also, one helper computer saves a tiny progress note to disk in a way that could get torn in half if the power dies at exactly the wrong moment. Both went on the fix-it list.

Part 3: BestChef, the cooking contest app

What is BestChef?

A cooking competition in your pocket. People cook a dish, film it, upload it, and others vote for the best one. The plan is to launch around the world in at least 7 languages. This week's theme: make it work for the whole planet, and make cheating impossible.

What did you build?

1. Teaching the app 21 languages. Almost a thousand phrases translated into 21 language files, including the boring but super important legal text. The computer now sends error CODES instead of English sentences, so your phone shows errors in YOUR language. Before, a French user could get scolded in English by the server.

2. Getting language details right. Arabic and Hebrew read right-to-left, so arrows and icons mirror. Polish and Russian have special counting rules (one thing, few things, many things are different words). We wired in the official rules for all of that, plus local time formats and emoji suggestions that understand dish names in many languages.

3. Dish names in your language. Dishes carry translations and search finds them in your language. Before, the app broke if a dish name was not written in English-style letters. Imagine a cooking app where you cannot type your own food's real name. Fixed.

4. A referee desk. A whole separate app for human moderators: reported videos, appeals, winners' proof photos, and promoting good videos into the public feed. If a wrongly removed video wins its appeal, it goes back up automatically.

5. Anti-cheating armor. Voting contests attract cheaters like picnics attract ants. We added limits on how many actions one person can do, a permanent fingerprint list of every vote-proof photo (so you cannot delete your vote and reuse the same photo), and blocks enforced by the server, not just hidden on your phone.

6. Uploads that survive bad Wi-Fi. Your video goes into a sturdy waiting line on the phone. Tunnel? Elevator? Dead battery? The upload continues later. A cleanup robot deletes leftover files so storage does not fill with junk.

7. The law stuff. Per-country age gates and a proper appeal flow for removed content, because different countries have different rules.

8. Fun stuff too. Comments on videos, bookmarks that follow you across devices, and confetti trophy moments when you win.

Any problems found?

Two honest ones. First, the robot judge that screens vote photos is still a PRETEND judge with fake scores, because the real photo-checking service is not connected yet. It is written on the fix-it list in capital letters, and the contest must not open to the public until the real judge is hired. Second, if two copies of the checking robot wake up at the same time, they can both grab the same photo and both stamp it. The stamping desk needs a "take a number" system.

Part 4: DoWork, the gym coach app

What is DoWork?

An app built around one real personal trainer. He films exercise videos, students subscribe to watch them, he coaches each student privately, and you control the video with your voice while working out, because your hands are busy holding weights.

What got built?

1. The trainer's studio. A profile page, a place to upload and manage videos, and QR invite codes he hands to people to make them his students.

2. Private coaching. A student films themselves exercising and sends it. The trainer replies with feedback pinned to exact seconds: "at 0:32 your back is bending." Like a teacher writing notes in the margin of your homework.

3. Voice control. Say "pause," "next," or "repeat" mid-exercise and the player obeys. The listening happens ON the phone; your voice is not shipped to some company's computer.

4. Real payments. Subscriptions run through the app store's real cash register. And the honesty rule again: after a purchase, the app asks OUR server "is this person really subscribed?" and only trusts that answer, because phones can be tricked.

5. Downloads with a permission recheck. Save videos for the no-signal gym basement, but the app rechecks permission at download AND at open. Cancel your subscription and the videos politely delete themselves.

6. A pile of honesty fixes. Raw computer errors get translated into friendly sentences before reaching humans. Fake GPS was replaced with the phone's real location. And we discovered the app had NO reachable login screen: the door existed but there was no hallway to it. We built the hallway.

7. Turning on the real backend. The production server was set up and verified live: database, six little server programs, notification hooks, and a demo trainer account with real videos.

Did the inspectors catch anything?

One good one. When you share a workout or send feedback and the server says NO (for example, your coaching link ended), the app treats every failure like a bad-Wi-Fi problem and says "Saved offline, will retry!" But some failures are the server genuinely refusing. Retrying will never help, and after enough retries the app quietly throws the message away. So the user was told "saved" about a thing that will never arrive. That is a lie by accident, and it is near the top of the fix-it list.

Part 5: MyNews, the newspaper app

What is MyNews?

A news app where reporters publish articles and volunteer editors help improve them, like Wikipedia manners meeting a newspaper. Readers can support reporters directly with tiny payments, and there are no ads.

What got built?

1. The foundation. MyNews became the 41st mini-app in the toolbox, with its own database tables and three math engines: one compares two versions of a text and finds the exact differences, one scores an editor's trustworthiness, one splits payments fairly.

2. Wax-seal publishing. Every article is signed with the reporter's secret key, like a wax seal on a letter. The server checks the seal BEFORE saving anything, so even the server cannot forge an article in your name. Plus a nice reader, reporter pages, and an RSS feed.

3. The editing desk. An editor cannot change your article. They can only SUGGEST: "in paragraph two, change X to Y, and here is my source." The reporter sees suggestions side by side and accepts, rejects, or counter-edits. Only the author can accept. Good suggestions earn credibility points. Newsrooms let teams share drafts with embargo labels ("secret until Friday").

Problems?

One worth telling. Creating a newsroom does two saves in a row: first the room, then adding you as its owner. If the second save fails, you get a ghost newsroom that exists but belongs to nobody, and you cannot see it. The fix is making both saves happen together or not at all, which databases know how to do. It is on the fix-it list before the real server gets turned on.

Part 6: The website, the big merge, and the inspections

What happened with the website?

The MyLife website learned to behave like a phone app: installable on your home screen, notch-safe layout, smarter Today dashboard. We also deleted a feature that showed made-up people with made-up likes in the workouts social tab. Fake friends are against the rules. Better an honest empty room than a fake party.

What is a "merge landing"?

Remember the four LEGO tables? Twice this week we carried everything onto the main table. Each time, before gluing anything, we ran the full robot checker army. The checkers caught three real bugs no single table could see: a counter that said 40 mini-apps when there were 41, a settings file accidentally made invisible by a hiding rule, and an upload waiting line that could shuffle two items uploaded in the same millisecond. All three fixed before landing. That is why we never merge blind.

And then you inspected everything?

Three layers. First, a harsh "would we let real customers use this TODAY?" review of all 8 products: the answer was zero out of eight, mostly because the money and legal plumbing is not connected yet, not because the code lies. Second, a deep world-launch inspection of BestChef that found real blockers, like demo food data that could show a fake "Organic Milk" to real users. Third, this sprint's code audit: we hired a second, completely different AI to read the four days of code with fresh eyes, then personally re-checked every serious claim before believing it. Two different brains catch more than one.

Why do you write down your own mistakes?

We keep a notebook called the errors log. Every real bug goes in with the date, where it lives, and whether it is fixed. Hiding mistakes makes software rot. Writing them down makes them chores. This week we added five new entries from the audit, and none of them get to hide.

So, in one sentence, what were the four days about?

We took four apps that had strong engines and gave them the rest of their bodies, honestly: private messages that do not lie about delivery, a cooking contest that speaks 21 languages and resists cheaters, a gym coach that takes real money and real voice commands, a newspaper with unforgeable wax seals, and then we let inspectors hunt for every flaw and wrote every flaw down.
