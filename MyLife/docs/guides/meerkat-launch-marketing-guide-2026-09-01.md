# Meerkat Launch Marketing Guide

Verified against MyLife commit `493b2549` and official market and platform sources on 2026-09-01. The HTML edition beside this file is the primary visual operating guide.

## Launch Objective

Launch Meerkat as the private social home for small, real communities that want richer structure than a group chat and more control than an advertising or engagement-driven platform.

The launch must sell a complete product truth:

> Private social, controlled by you.

Meerkat gives a group private spaces, channels and posts nobody can forge, private messages only the two of you can read, locked files, a personal library, syncing between your own devices, a choice of who runs the servers, and a separate anonymous way to take part in public.

And it does not hand the group a fixed shape. The owner composes the community from signed blocks and channel kinds, members build pages, profiles, and decoration on top of it, and the same community can carry an optional public front. That freedom is the differentiator no competitor has, and it is the second thing the launch says, never the first: people arrive for a home they control and stay for a place they built.

## Current Launch Truth

### Safe to demonstrate

- The five-tab mobile experience: Feed, Communities, Public, Messages, and Me.
- Community creation and signed invite preview.
- Channel messages, posts, threads, reactions, mentions, pages, files, libraries, themes, roles, and archive behavior.
- The creation layer: the owner layout editor with its block palette, live preview, and copyable layout template code; the nine channel kinds; member canvases on the commons, channel toppers, posts, the pixel board, per-community profile pages, and member-built pages an owner promotes to a tab; asset packs, badges, personas, milestones, polls, and guestbooks.
- The custom theme editor, including generating a palette from one color and sharing a theme as a code or QR.
- The six community starting templates: `Family Space`, `Media Library`, `Club`, `Course Hub`, `Newsroom`, and `Blank`.
- Friend-code exchange, safety-code comparison, one-to-one and group direct messages.
- Real manual relay sessions and development-build LAN sessions.
- Protected share links, the Share Inbox you reach from another app's share button, the personal Library, and encrypted backup destinations.
- One-time `$4.99` private unlock and the optional `$4.99` monthly hosted service.
- Owner attachment and removal of a healthy HTTPS community server.

### Demonstrate only with visible prerequisites

- Reading and posting in the public area need their servers switched on and reachable. Without them the app says so on screen, which is what a demo audience will see.
- Calls and rooms require a full native build and live call service.
- LAN, Nearby, WebRTC transport, Bluetooth Low Energy wake, and native playback require the corresponding full-build modules.
- Scheduled background sync and data-only push wake require the development-build flag and deployed push path.
- Storage providers require live credentials, provider setup, and restore evidence.

### Never claim

- A friend is online because a connection server is reachable.
- Content was delivered, read, published, or backed up without the real receipt, approved host result, or verified timestamp.
- Public services are live in a build where the app reports that they are off or not connected.
- Creator membership, storefront, or zero-fee creator checkout is shipping. These are documented creator-rail plans, not the current product. The storefront and membership-tier blocks can be placed in a layout today and render an honest unavailable line; never demonstrate them as a working shop.
- A Meerkat community can be published as a website at a custom domain. The open-web page renderer is a documented plan, not a shipped capability.
- Members can run their own code, scripts, or plugins inside Meerkat. They cannot, deliberately, and that is a stated safety property rather than a missing feature.
- Customer, revenue, retention, or conversion figures that have not been measured.

## Beachhead Audience

### Primary: private organizers

Small-group owners who currently coordinate on Discord, WhatsApp, Slack, or a patchwork of chat, files, and shared documents:

- local clubs and mutual-aid circles;
- book, film, outdoor, maker, and study groups;
- family and household networks;
- privacy and self-hosting communities;
- independent organizers who want to own availability and presentation.

Their trigger is not abstract privacy. It is the need for a durable home with channels, posts, files, pages, private messages, and a clear owner-controlled connection model.

### Secondary: creator-owned communities

Independent writers, educators, artists, podcasters, and niche experts who need a member space today and may later use the planned creator rails. Launch messaging must sell the current community product, not promise an unfinished commerce system.

### Tertiary: builders and customizers

People who modify games, keep a personal site, ran a MySpace or Geocities page, or use SpaceHey, mmm.page, straw.page, or Hotglue today. They are the smallest of the three audiences and the loudest. They will find the creation layer without being told about privacy, and they produce the artifacts (shared layout codes, theme codes, screenshots of built pages) that make the product legible to everyone else. Reach them through the work, not through claims: show real built communities.

### Quaternary: privacy-forward families and friend groups

People who want direct messages, shared files, local libraries, safety-code verification, and a one-time purchase without an always-required social account.

## Positioning

### Category

The private app a group runs its whole life in.

### Positioning statement

For small groups that want a real home without handing over who they are and what they post, Meerkat is one private app that holds chat, posts, pages, files, and a library, syncs between devices, and lets the group build it into whatever shape it actually needs. Unlike platforms built to keep you scrolling, Meerkat keeps your identity on your own phone, lets the group choose who runs its servers and what it looks like, and only ever shows you things it can actually prove.

### Message pillars

1. **One place that holds everything.** Channels, posts, pages, a library, files, colors, private messages, and a way to organize it, all in one app instead of five.
2. **Privacy is how it is built, not a promise.** Your phone makes its own key and keeps it. Messages are locked before they leave. Files are locked on the device. You check a friend with five emoji. The sign-in you use for public posting is kept completely apart from all of it.
3. **Control, without pretending.** Use the free connection server, pay for ours, or point it at your own. The app always tells you the truth about what is reachable and what is not.
4. **A place you build, not a template you put up with.** Whoever runs the group builds it out of ready-made blocks. Everyone in it can add pages, a profile, and decoration on top. Everything carries its maker's name, and nothing anyone adds can reach out to the internet.

### Primary message stack

- Hero: `Private social, controlled by you.`
- Subhead: `A real home for the people you actually know. Group spaces, private messages, pages, files, and a library, all locked on your own phone.`
- Primary call to action during pilot: `Join the TestFlight pilot`.
- Primary call to action at general availability: `Get Meerkat for $4.99`.
- Secondary call to action: `See how your data moves`.
- Creation-layer line, for the audiences that respond to it: `Build it into whatever your group actually needs.`

### Proof language

Use:

- `$4.99 once. No subscription.`
- `$4.99 a month if you want us to run the servers. Optional.`
- `Everything private works without ever paying again.`
- `Check five emoji with someone before you trust them. If they do not match, someone is in the way.`
- `A server answering does not mean your friend is online, so we never say they are.`
- `You sign in separately to post publicly, and that sign-in is never linked to your private side.`
- `Sixteen building blocks, nine kinds of channel. Your group does not have to look like anyone else's.`
- `Every sticker, page, and post carries the name of whoever made it, and nobody can fake that.`
- `Nothing anyone adds can reach out to the internet. Colours come from a set list, and pictures live on your device instead of being loaded from somewhere else.`
- `Anyone can read what a group publishes, without signing up. Nobody at all can read the private side.`

Avoid:

- `Unhackable`, `anonymous`, `serverless`, `zero metadata`, `always online`, or `fully decentralized` as blanket claims.
- `Delivered`, `read`, `published`, `backed up`, or `connected` unless the product shows the corresponding real result.
- `Free forever` for the complete product.

## The Creation Layer and the Public Face

This is the part of Meerkat nobody else has, and it is the second thing you say, never the first. Open with the home. Win the argument with the freedom.

### Why it sells

Someone running a group buys a home. What keeps them, and what makes them tell a friend, is that the home is theirs to shape. This is a well-worn pattern outside our category: Skyrim, Grand Theft Auto, and Super Mario Maker all outlived their own content because players could build what the studio never would, and SpaceHey signed up roughly 1.9 million people offering almost nothing except the freedom to design your own page. People still pay for this today, on mmm.page, straw.page, and Hotglue.

Everything Meerkat gets compared to gets this wrong in one of two ways. Discord, Slack, and Circle decide the shape for you and sell you accent colors and their own templates. Signal, SimpleX, Session, and Briar do not let you decorate anything, because a list of messages is the whole product. Nobody has done both, because letting people design things has always been a security nightmare.

### What makes it defensible, in one sentence per property

- **Everything carries its maker's name.** Every block, page, sticker, and post is stamped by whoever made it, and anything that fails the check is thrown away before it is even saved. Decoration you can trace to a person becomes something people compete over, rather than anonymous wallpaper.
- **Nothing anyone adds can reach the internet.** People pick colors and shapes from a set list instead of typing in code, and pictures live on the device as locked files instead of being loaded from an address. There is simply nowhere for a member's creation to put a web address, which is what killed every earlier attempt at this: no way to phone home with your data, no invisible tracking pixel, and no swapping the image for something else after a moderator approved it.
- **Anything can be undone.** Nothing is ever overwritten, only added to, so wrecking someone's page is a thing you undo rather than a thing you fight about. And everyone has their own controls over what their phone will show them.
- **Nobody can fake the app's own markers.** No amount of decoration can draw a fake verified tick, a fake lock, or a fake "connected" light. The app draws those itself, outside anything a member can touch, and there are tests that fail if that ever stops being true.

### How to say it

Say: *the group decides what the app is.* One group can be a forum, a photo wall, a video channel, an events board, a reading room, a pixel board, or a wall of pages its members designed, all out of the same app, with nothing to install and no server to run.

Do not say *plugins*, *apps*, *scripts*, *extensions*, or *code*. Meerkat runs nobody's code on anybody's phone, on purpose. That is worth saying out loud when it comes up: it is the reason this is safe and every earlier version of it was not.

### The public face

Meerkat has a public side, and it is a real part of the product rather than a footnote. Whoever runs a group can publish out of it. Anyone can read that with no account, no profile, and nothing following them around. The owner can hand out an open invitation that turns a reader into a member. The public side and the private side are walled off from each other by how the app is built: when you post publicly you carry a pass that proves you are allowed to, without saying who you are, and nothing the app stores or sends ever puts your sign-in next to your key.

The honest way to put it: **one group, private on the inside, with an optional public front, both built in the same editor.** A group can stay completely hidden, publish a page anyone can read, or open its door to newcomers, and none of those choices moves the private content anywhere.

One real limit, and say it in every demo: the public side needs its servers switched on and reachable. Without them the app says `Public accounts are off in this build` or `The public feed is not connected in this build`, and that is exactly what your audience will see on screen. Never put that screen up and talk about what it would do if it worked.

### What is not shipped, and how to handle the question

When someone asks about selling access or running a shop, the honest answer is: you can already put a shop block and a membership block on a page, they currently say so plainly instead of working, and the rest of it (paid tiers, checkout codes, delivering a locked file to a buyer, showing different blocks to different people, and turning a group into a website at your own domain) is a written engineering plan. Say *planned*. Never show a placed block and let anyone think it is a working shop.

## Competitive Frame

Marketing should compare the operating model, not attack competitors.

| Alternative | What it is good at | Meerkat launch contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Mature private messaging, calls, group links, nonprofit trust. Signal says messages and calls are always end-to-end encrypted. | Meerkat adds owner-structured communities, pages, libraries, protected content, and selectable infrastructure. Do not claim Signal lacks private messaging. |
| WhatsApp Communities | Familiar groups and communities with end-to-end encrypted personal messages and calls. | Meerkat does not require a phone-number social identity and adds local libraries, signed community structure, and owner-selected servers. |
| Discord | Rich community UX, voice, integrations, and broad reach. Its privacy policy covers stored messages, content, activity, and advertising interactions. | Meerkat is designed around local identity, encrypted private content, no advertising model, and no product analytics. Discord remains stronger for scale and integrations. |
| Slack | Mature work collaboration. Its free plan has 90-day history; paid plans are per active user. | Meerkat uses a one-time private unlock plus optional owner hosting, not per-seat workspace pricing. Slack remains stronger for enterprise administration and integrations. |
| Element and Matrix | Open protocol, federation, self-hosting, and end-to-end encryption. | Meerkat aims for a consumer-first integrated device, community, library, and sealed-content experience. Matrix remains stronger as an ecosystem and protocol network. |
| Freeform page and profile products (SpaceHey, mmm.page, straw.page, Hotglue) | Genuine creative freedom and an active audience for it. SpaceHey has reached roughly 1.9 million registered users. | Meerkat offers comparable freedom with signed authorship, sealed assets, and encrypted private content underneath. Do not claim these products are unsafe; say that they were never built to carry private community content. |
| SimpleX, Session, and Briar | Strong privacy-first communication, identifier minimization, decentralized routing, or offline transports. | Meerkat differentiates through a broader community home: posts, pages, themes, libraries, public reading boundary, and optional hosted ownership. These products remain important privacy benchmarks. |
| Circle and Patreon | Polished creator communities, memberships, payments, and audience operations. Circle starts at `$89/month`; Patreon applies platform fees to new creators. | Meerkat launches first on privacy and community ownership. Planned creator rails must not be marketed as shipped until checkout, entitlement, storefront, and support operations are live. |

## Launch System

### Owned channels

- Product site with the positioning, pricing, architecture explainer, user guide, privacy policy, terms, support address, and service status.
- Email list with explicit consent and a plain unsubscribe path.
- Founder launch note and technical architecture note.
- Demo video and captioned product walkthrough.
- App Store and Play Store listings.
- Public changelog, availability matrix, and known-limitations page.

### Rented channels

- Apple App Store and Google Play discovery.
- Product Hunt for a concentrated launch-day proof package.
- Hacker News for the architecture and owner-control story.
- Two focused communities where self-hosting or privacy infrastructure is already discussed. Follow each community's promotion rules.
- Founder-led posts on no more than two social networks that can be maintained consistently.

### Borrowed channels

- Privacy and security reviewers with a threat-model brief and a reproducible tester path.
- Self-hosting writers, podcasters, and maintainers.
- Five to ten small-community organizers who can document a real migration or pilot.
- Independent developers who can review the sync and server boundary without receiving private user data.

## Required Launch Assets

1. **Product page.** One audience, one promise, three proof pillars, exact pricing, five current screenshots, an availability table, and a real call to action.
2. **App Store package.** Final icon, 30-character name, 30-character subtitle, keyword set, description, privacy URL, support URL, review notes, purchase metadata, age rating, screenshots for every required device size, and a working demo account only if review requires one. Apple documents the name, subtitle, and privacy URL fields in [App information](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-information).
3. **Screenshot set.** Use real signed-build captures of Communities, a channel, Messages, protected sharing, and Connection status. Show sample data and never composite a status the build did not produce.
4. **Demo video.** Ninety seconds: create a community, invite a friend, compare safety code, send a private message, add a file, run a manual sync, and show the session ledger.
5. **Trust packet.** Privacy policy, threat model, data-flow diagram, encryption and metadata boundary, account-isolation explanation, data deletion explanation, vulnerability-reporting contact, and supported-build matrix.
6. **Press kit.** Product description, founder bio, approved screenshots, icon files, architecture diagram, pricing card, availability statement, and media contact.
7. **Reviewer kit.** Two-device setup, test identities, fresh friend codes, exact relay phrase instructions, expected successful states, common failure recovery, and a private issue-report channel.
8. **Support kit.** Public help center, complete user guide, incident template, refund and restore-purchase guide, backup recovery guide, and service-status page.

Apple requires user-generated-content apps to include filtering, reporting, blocking, and published contact information. It also requires in-app purchase for digital functionality under the relevant store rules. Verify the final submission against the current [App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/).

## Launch Sequence

Every stage prepares the whole product, not a cut-down version. These are checkpoints for evidence, not permission to ship less.

### T-30 to T-22: evidence lock

- Freeze the claim matrix against the release commit.
- Verify every service endpoint, purchase, restore, delete, backup, report, block, and support path in the release build.
- Capture the five canonical screenshots from the signed build.
- Complete the privacy policy, terms, support address, and status page.
- Run two-device relay and LAN evidence; run a restore drill.
- Produce the reviewer kit and incident-response contact tree.

Done when every public claim has a release-build screenshot, test result, or service-health record.

### T-21 to T-15: pilot proof

- Recruit five to ten community organizers, each with a real group and named use case.
- Onboard each organizer using the complete user guide.
- Collect opt-in interviews at day 1 and day 7.
- Resolve launch-blocking confusion, crashes, purchase failures, delivery ambiguity, and backup failures.
- Record only aggregate operational facts that do not violate the zero-analytics product position.

Done when organizers can create or join, add a person, send content, sync, and recover without founder intervention.

### T-14 to T-8: store and press preparation

- Upload the final product page, privacy details, screenshots, review notes, and in-app purchase metadata.
- Submit the signed release candidate for review.
- Give reviewers the trust packet under embargo.
- Schedule the founder note, technical note, demo, and help-center publication.
- Prepare three alternate App Store product-page treatments for later measurement. Apple allows testing up to three alternatives through [product page optimization](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page-optimization/).

Done when the store listing is complete, the build is reviewable, and every external link is live.

### T-7 to T-1: coordinated preview

- Publish the launch date only after review and operational readiness are known.
- Send one preview to the consented email list.
- Give pilot organizers a shareable launch card with no tracking inside Meerkat.
- Confirm support coverage, service alerts, rollback owner, and incident templates.
- Re-run purchase, restore, relay, invite, moderation, deletion, and backup smoke tests.

Done when the launch operator can answer who owns every incident class and can reach them.

### Launch day

- Publish the product page, guide, trust packet, founder note, demo, and store link together.
- Post one architecture-focused Hacker News submission and one Product Hunt launch, each tailored to the venue.
- Invite pilot organizers to share their real use case, without scripted testimonials.
- Monitor App Store Connect, RevenueCat aggregates, server health, support intake, and crash reports.
- Update the public status page for any material incident. Never mask a degraded service with marketing copy.

Done when every public call to action reaches the live listing and the operational dashboard is green.

### T+1 to T+30: learn and prove

- Publish fixes and known limitations in plain language.
- Interview activated organizers and people who abandoned setup.
- Test App Store screenshots and product-page variants using aggregate store tools.
- Publish a technical postmortem for any material incident.
- Turn real, consented organizer outcomes into case studies.

Done when the next release and campaign brief cite observed evidence instead of assumptions.

## Measurement Without Product Analytics

Meerkat's product position is zero analytics and zero telemetry. Marketing measurement must preserve that promise.

### Allowed aggregate sources

- App Store Connect impressions, product-page views, downloads, and proceeds.
- RevenueCat aggregate purchase and restore outcomes, without joining them to Meerkat identities.
- Service uptime, capacity, and error aggregates that contain no account identifier beside a persona or device identifier.
- Consent-based email campaign totals and privacy-preserving external link parameters.
- Opt-in interviews and surveys stored outside Meerkat's private identity graph.
- Support volume by issue category after removing personal content.

### Launch scorecard

Track:

- store page view to download rate;
- download to completed onboarding rate from opt-in pilot observation, not hidden telemetry;
- purchase and restore success rate;
- organizer time to create or join a community;
- percentage of pilot organizers completing a real two-device or two-person session;
- invite failure, connection-health failure, and backup verification incident counts;
- day-7 organizer retention from consented check-ins;
- support response and resolution time;
- service uptime and saturation.

Do not create a cross-system identifier that links store, billing, account, persona, device, community, or private-message activity.

## Launch Roles

- **Founder:** final positioning, pricing changes, legal decisions, App Store agreements, financial decisions, public statements, and incident severity decisions.
- **Release engineer:** signed build, environment validation, store upload, release manifest, rollback, and service-health evidence.
- **Security owner:** threat model, vulnerability intake, privacy claim review, key and account boundary checks, and incident investigation.
- **Community safety owner:** reports, blocking, moderation operations, contact path, escalation, and response coverage.
- **Support owner:** help center, purchase restoration, backup recovery, ticket triage, and status updates.
- **Marketing owner:** asset production, launch calendar, channel execution, consent records, and claim-matrix compliance.

A person may hold multiple roles, but every role must have one named owner and one reachable backup before launch.

## Go or No-Go Marketing Gate

Marketing may announce general availability only when:

- the signed build passes purchase and restore on a real store account;
- the release build contains the intended connection-server configuration and passes a real health check;
- two-device relay sync, invite, direct message, community message, protected file, and session ledger are evidenced;
- moderation report, block, safety contact, privacy policy, account deletion, and local-data deletion are tested;
- storage backup and restore are proven on each marketed provider;
- every marketed public, call, background, and native-transport capability has live evidence, or is removed from launch copy;
- store metadata, screenshots, support URL, privacy URL, and in-app purchase metadata match the release build;
- support, security, safety, incident, rollback, and service-status owners are scheduled;
- no marketing artifact contains unverified traction, market-size, delivery, availability, or security claims.

If one gate fails, fix the capability or remove the claim before launch. Do not relabel an absent dependency as a temporary success.
